# Rust and lab collection performance plan

Measure the installed Windows game and the Linux lab independently. For the game, aim for more completed search trajectories within the existing turn allowance and lower response and frame-time tails. For the lab, aim for **15% less wall time through accepted collection** with 320 PPO games and the current teacher workload. That would be about **92–95 s** against the two g2090 export samples. Then test whether the saved time can support **40 or 48 teacher training games** while collecting the same 320 PPO games.

The speed comparisons keep the model, rules, feature schema, reward, search settings, opening and cotton sampling, and PPO behavior probabilities fixed. Use the committed lab recipe for each comparison. Test a higher teacher table count separately because it changes the pool of training examples.

## Implementation and Windows results

The changes remove the unused ordinary CPU tensor serialization, batch teacher logit checks by candidate width, release teacher CPUs before finalization when the queue is empty, and let ordinary publishers prepare fresh PPO data from the checked family they just sealed. The cold replay path still verifies stored bytes. The Linux CPU allocator now visits physical cores before SMT siblings within the available affinity mask. The lab recipe remains at **32 teacher training games** and four held-outs when due.

## What the current measurements show

### Lab results through g2280

The g2280 export carries compact summaries for iterations 2150–2277 plus full reports for 2278 and 2279. The trainer restarted at 2150, 2240 and 2250 (CPU preparation worker PIDs). [g2280 reports](../results/joint7-v11-2-g1190-restart-3-g2280-20261114T181904011113Z).

**PPO.** Every iteration runs 46 optimizer steps, and the update itself takes 20–25 s. The variable part is `cpu_preparation.transfer_seconds`, the main-thread consumption of prepared batches.

| PPO transfer | Iterations | PPO time |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| 3.2–4.3 s | 2150–2164, 2241–2246, 2263–2264, roughly a third of 2165–2240 | 33–47 s |
| 20–33 s | most iterations since 2165, before and after the revision | 55–79 s |
| 185.5, 189.7, 232.0 s | 2274, 2269, 2273 | 241.9, 240.7, 276.9 s |

Each pass staged 8.3 GB of inputs and wrote 28.7 GB of padded batches as files under `artifacts/` on the lab's data disk. Each consumed batch was then read back, copied, pinned and unlinked. Under writeback, unlinking a file waits for its pages to reach the disk, so consuming 28.7 GB ran at disk speed: about 1 GB/s typically and 120–155 MB/s in the three outliers. Staging doubled from about 9 s to 17–23 s in the same way. The pattern started at 2165, before the collection revision, and the revision didn't change this path.

The fix keeps these transport files in RAM-backed `/dev/shm` when tmpfs and available memory both have at least 32 GiB free, and in the previous folder otherwise. It removes the folders of dead trainers, and copies each batch once straight into pinned memory. The bytes, order and checks are unchanged. The expected PPO time is about 33–37 s with no disk-speed outliers.

**Collection.** Ordinary collection improved: the ordinary pool took 67.3 and 70.9 s at 2278/2279, against 87.2 and 94.6 s at 2238/2239. Replay preparation CPU fell from about 470 to 156–161 worker-seconds. Teacher collection did not: its wall time stayed about 107 s, so ordinary collection plus the teacher wait is still about 107 s (106.0/107.1 s at 2238/2239, 106.9/107.9 s at 2278/2279).

| Per teacher table, median | 2238/2239 | 2278/2279 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Native search | 64–66 s | 38 s |
| ONNX forward | 27–28 s | 15 s |
| After search | 23–28 s | 58–61 s |
| Table | 89–92 s | 97–99 s |

After the native speedup, the tables wait on Python instead of searching. Individual long-search label turns waited 26–36 s after their search, almost all of them 10–80 s into the table, while ordinary collection was also running in the trainer process. A labelled turn costs about 40–100 ms of trainer CPU: row validation, the JSON-schema wire check and feature encoding, plus freezing. With 36 tables those checks hold one interpreter lock for about 55–65 s per iteration, on top of ordinary coordination and table publication.

The fix runs label-row validation, schema checks and encoding for streamed fixed-count tables in four persistent spawned helper processes. Each table always goes to the same helper, so its validation cache stays warm. The trainer keeps its frozen rows, turn, identity and lineage checks, the batched logit parity check and publication. Freezing also copies scalar leaves and all-scalar lists without a call per element, about half the cost. On a local fixture table the helper path produced byte-identical encoded rows, and trainer-side work per labelled turn roughly halved, with the encoding off the interpreter lock entirely. With the lock free, tables should end close to their 38–40 s native search, and the ordinary pool can claim their released CPUs. The combined lab gain is still to be measured.

**PPG.** Matched by optimizer steps, the PPG value phase is not slower: training takes 3.5–4 s at 0 steps, about 8 s at 16 and about 15 s at 58–59, as before the revision. The search-value phase takes 3.2–5.4 s, against 5.2–6.4 s at 2238/2239. The higher recent values come from more scheduled steps (59 at both 2278 and 2279) and from `replay_open_and_index_seconds` spikes. These spikes take 9–15 s instead of about 1 s and hit about one iteration in four both before and after the revision, including 2277–2279. Opening the previous age reads small `role`, `table` and `game` columns across every row of its 1.6 GB `critic.npy`. When that file has left the page cache, this becomes a full disk read. The value phase now asks the kernel to read that age ahead (`posix_fadvise` WILLNEED) from a background thread while PPO runs.

### Lab results through g2250

The g2250 export includes one ordinary iteration and one with four held-outs and periodic maintenance. All 320 ordinary shards in each iteration used publisher-side preparation. Preparation worker CPU fell from roughly 471–473 seconds in the g2240 exports to 156–157 seconds. Combined collection took 90.82 and 108.40 seconds, compared with 107.55 and 108.67 seconds at g2240. The cotton and teacher sampling recipes also changed between exports. [g2240 reports](../results/joint7-v11-2-g1190-restart-3-g2240-20261114T060652073419Z), [g2250 reports](../results/joint7-v11-2-g1190-restart-3-g2250-20261114T075328979389Z).

On iteration 2249, a median teacher game spent 38.2 seconds in native search and 57.1 seconds after search. Native post-search preparation accounted for about 0.8 seconds per game. The remainder mostly reflects the turn handoff and wait for the Python acknowledgement. The ordinary pool finished in 76.3 seconds, and the teacher barrier finished in 106.6 seconds. Cleanup added 78.0 seconds and evaluation added 63.2 seconds. [Teacher collection trace](../results/joint7-v11-2-g1190-restart-3-g2250-20261114T075328979389Z/iteration-2249/teacher-shadow/collection.json), [iteration report](../results/joint7-v11-2-g1190-restart-3-g2250-20261114T075328979389Z/iteration-2249/report.json).

The CPU count worker now streams turns through bounded pipes while Python verifies them in order. Its final frame reports the count of value rows already delivered. A table still needs complete rows and independent logit parity before publication. Live immutable root records reuse their turn validation during return fitting, and recovered tables take the full validation path. Cleanup hashes each file just before quarantining it and again before deletion. The compact diagnostic archive keeps teacher VP and aggregate results and drops per-turn traces duplicated in the collection reports. On the g2250 archive this reduced uncompressed JSON from 398 MB to 135 MB. One matched six-table Windows probe reduced median post-search time per table from 2.02 to 1.80 seconds with identical training data, while total wall time rose from 53.2 to 57.4 seconds as native search took longer. A local 256 MiB cleanup fixture showed no wall-time gain. The net Linux timing remains to be measured. ## 2. Lab collection and scheduling

### Shorten the teacher tail

1. **Batch the existing independent logit checks across finishing tables.** `TurnVerifier.settle` takes a shared model lock and runs a separate check per table. Send its already-prepared immutable rows to one bounded verifier for the frozen model. Group by legal candidate width and cap both rows and padded cells. Flush on a short bounded delay or immediately when the last table is waiting. Use the existing PyTorch policy path and tolerances, restore row order by job/row identity, and verify every row before sealing its table. Measure enqueue, collate, device forward, device-to-host transfer, and final publication separately. Send one model check failure through the existing cancellation path. [Turn verifier](../src/brass_ai/rust_backend/lab_teacher_finalization.py), [logit checking](../src/brass_ai/recovery/teacher_labels.py), [policy batching](../src/brass_ai/recovery/teacher_term.py).
2. **Stream the remaining teacher finalization where the trace supports it.** Stage verified table output while other games are in search. Preserve deterministic job order and atomic generation sealing for policy labels and search-value rows. Compare cache encoding, gzip, hashing and file writes individually before changing the format. Report separate timestamps for last native search, last logit verification, and teacher policy/value admission. [Teacher collector](../src/brass_ai/rust_backend/lab_teacher.py), [table cache](../src/brass_ai/rust_backend/lab_shadow.py), [teacher admission](../src/brass_ai/recovery/teacher_phase.py).
3. **If ONNX remains a major cost, test batching independent games.** Prototype a bounded CPU batch of two to four leaves with matching model identity and similar candidate widths. Keep each game's search state and decision order private and route outputs by the exact request identity. Measure queue time, padded cells and table tails before increasing batch size. Treat a change to within-tree backup order, inference precision or search quotas as a separate experiment.

### Reduce ordinary PPO collection work

1. **Measure IPC by operation before changing the decision protocol.** Start with the unused tensor serialization fix. Compare the Rust transition timer to the wider `native_step_seconds` span and sample `infer`, `append_compact`, forced acknowledgements and completion export. If round trips remain hot, fuse advancing a confirmed action with preparing the next decision only when its context is already bound. Keep Python sampling, the current forced-action records, the full mixed `behavior.probability`, and second-develop eligibility. Check sampled choices and replay likelihood against the existing path. [Collection loop](../src/brass_ai/rust_backend/lab_collection.py), [ordinary CPU inference](../src/brass_ai/rust_backend/lab_cpu_inference.py), [transport](../src/brass_ai/rust_backend/lab_transport.py).
2. **Reduce the publish-and-read round trip.** The publisher already verifies and assembles each family. For fresh families, prototype handing the replay reader a compact, immutable view of that verified data after durable publication. Reuse exact feature buffers and row bindings through a bounded read-only handoff. Keep independent hash and schema checks at the process boundary and the full cold read for disk recovery. Compare this with the current reader path before taking on a format change. Once reads are cheaper, measure whether economy, cotton and exploration summaries can share a single record traversal and produce identical reports. 3. **Check model and process locality.** Record cache loads, evictions and initialization by PPO table type. Try routing frozen historical jobs to an available worker that already has their ONNX sessions, using existing model-cohort scheduling where applicable and a stable tie-breaker. Preserve the job-to-opponent assignment and final order. Test a bounded warm-worker pool across iterations only if actual spawn or model loads remain material.
